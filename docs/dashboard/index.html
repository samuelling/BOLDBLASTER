BOLDBLASTER: what can a video game recover from visual cortex?

An open fMRI dataset of people playing a game would let anyone fit their model to it. Each experiment here plants known tuning in simulated voxels, generates BOLD with realistic noise, and fits it back from gameplay as one would with real data, so we can score what a game recovers, how much play it takes, and how it compares with dedicated mapping experiments. In short: gameplay is a weaker per-parameter mapper than an hour of the right dedicated experiment, but one dataset serves every model at once, and the game matters. Real games beat the purpose-built raycaster, and an hour of a realistic shooter recovers contrast and attention tuning better than two hours of the raycaster.

Eight experiments at a glance

Every value is Spearman r between planted and fitted parameters across simulated voxels, so 1 means the voxels are put in exactly the right order. Treat differences under about 0.1 as noise. Open any experiment for what it looked like, its charts and its caveats.

Can pRFs be mapped from free gameplay?

What we did

Experiment 2 · Rounds 2–3

Can one gameplay dataset serve many models at once?

What we did

Each row is one design: a dedicated experiment built for that parameter, the game with and without design levers, and up to 20 hours across ten levels. Dots are Spearman r; whiskers are bootstrap 95% intervals over voxels (not computed for the non-visual populations). Round 2 plants seven V1 tuning parameters (SF peak and bandwidth, contrast response, surround suppression, a transient channel, attention gain) plus numerosity, self-motion and striatal reward populations; round 3 adds design levers: covert-attention cues and textures that decorrelate SF from contrast.

RESULT

Gameplay does best where a game naturally varies a quantity a lot or produces many events: surround suppression (objects of every size against every background), transient responses (saccades, camera turns) and reward outcomes. Against dedicated experiments of realistic length (rows at the bottom, and section 02) it loses on SF tuning, attention and self-motion, and roughly ties on contrast response and numerosity. Parameters that interact (surround, transient weight, CRF exponent) are poorly recovered one experiment at a time however long it runs, so fitting them jointly is what matters.

Experiment 4 · Round 5

How does gameplay compare with dedicated experiments of realistic length?

What we did

Real pRF, pSFT and pCRF mapping takes 30–60+ minutes, not 10. Here every dedicated experiment is run for 10, 30 and 60 minutes, fitted on its own and as a joint battery (all five V1 experiments, up to 5 h), and set against gameplay on the same 120 voxels. Uses the parameter picked here.

RESULT

A fair baseline changes the headline. Hour for hour, a dedicated experiment beats an hour of gameplay on — of 7 V1 measures (SF peak, effective c50, attention gain); none is clearly better from an hour of play (gameplay wins: —). At a matched 5 h budget the full dedicated battery wins — (SF peak and bandwidth, attention), gameplay wins — (surround suppression: r = — vs —), and — tie, with transient weight leaning toward gameplay. Longer dedicated scans mostly help the noise-limited parameters; the one-at-a-time fits of transient weight, surround and SF bandwidth stay poor at 60 minutes because the other parameters are held at defaults.

Experiment 5 · Round 6

Attention dynamics, gaze tolerance of large receptive fields, and one game lever

What we did

Three follow-ups on the round-5 baseline: can gameplay recover the time course (not just the gain) of attention; do large, foveally-biased receptive fields buy gaze tolerance; and does tightening one game cue actually help.

RESULT

Attention gain is weakly recoverable from gameplay and improves with duration (oracle gaze: — → — → — at 20/40/60 min), but its rise and decay time constants are not: the attention term is only a few percent of the core visual drive's size, too small a ripple for its shape to come through. The dedicated block design is worse off on principle, not just power: a block has no temporal shape inside it for a decay time to show up in. A large, foveally-biased ventral population loses only — Spearman r to realistic eye-tracker gaze at 40 minutes, against — for V1 at the same duration — gaze tolerance does scale with receptive-field size, as hypothesized. Tightening the covert cue's spacing (6–10 s → 3–5 s) did not help attention-gain recovery (— versus — for the original schedule), plausibly an fMRI design-efficiency cost of packing events too close together.

The attention term is only a few percent of the core drive's SD (median voxel) — a small multiplicative ripple riding on a much larger signal plus comparable measurement noise. That is a genuine low-SNR finding, confirmed by comparing the achieved fit against plugging in the true (a, rise, decay) into the same mismatched core: both give nearly the same prediction correlation, meaning the ridge is flat (non-identifiable), not under-optimized.

Caveats

Gameplay's irregular event timing is the one of the two designs that could in principle see transient dynamics — it just doesn't see them well in this model, because the effect size is small, not because events are too regular.

What it shows

This is the design's ceiling, not a fitted result: it says what information is there to find, independent of noise or optimizer behavior.

Experiment 6 · Round 7

Spatiotemporal pRFs: can gameplay separate sustained from transient channels?

What we did

After Kim/Kupers/Grill-Spector's compressive spatiotemporal pRF: the old instantaneous transient term is replaced by two channels with their own memory — a sustained low-pass of the centre drive (τs) and a transient low-pass of the frame-difference drive (τt), mixed by a per-voxel weight ws. A dedicated SEQ-SIM design (two full-field probes, shown together or apart at several gaps) stands in for Kupers' own sequential-vs-simultaneous bar paradigm as the controlled comparison.

RESULT

The channel-mixing weight (ws) is the one piece of this model gameplay gets any traction on at all (oracle gaze: — → — → — at 20/40/60 min); both time constants stay low throughout (τs oracle, 60 min: —; τt: —). The dedicated SEQ-SIM design, run at the same durations, does not rescue this — if anything it does no better than gameplay (ws, 60 min: —). The likely reason is not a failure of timing control but of duty cycle: the sustained and transient channels are at least as correlated with each other under SEQ-SIM's brief, mostly-blank probes as they are under gameplay's continuous content (— vs —), so the controlled design buys no extra separation between ws and the overall response gain, while paying a real cost in total driven signal.

What am I looking at?

What it shows

A ground-truth check, not a fit: filter the centre drive (sustained channel) and the frame-difference drive (transient channel) at the population-median time constants, under each design's actual visual input, and correlate them over time, per voxel (median across voxels shown). A high correlation means the two channels move together — ws (their relative weight) trades off against the overall response gain and becomes hard to pin down, independent of noise or amount of data.

How to read it

SEQ-SIM's channels are no less collinear than gameplay's — the sequential-vs-simultaneous manipulation, in this full-field, mostly-blank form, doesn't buy the separation Kupers' retinotopic bar version does. Gameplay's continuous, always-on content drives both channels throughout the run, which turns out to cost about as much independence as SEQ-SIM's sparse on/off probes, while delivering far more total driven signal — which is plausibly why gameplay edges SEQ-SIM out on ws rather than losing to it.

Caveats

This diagnostic explains the channel-mixing (ws) result; it says nothing about why the time constants themselves (τs, τt) stay low under every design — that looks like the same low-SNR story as round 6's attention time constants, a small-ripple-on-a-big-signal problem rather than a design-identifiability one.

Experiment 7 · Round 8

Does the game matter? Three real games against the raycaster

What we did

Every earlier round used BOLDBLASTER's own raycaster. Here the stimulus is three of our real games, each played by a bot for twelve 5-minute runs and shown on the same 24 × 18° screen at 192 × 144 px:

  • Betamax: the Mayflower Beach battle, played forward with no rewind and no taking bodies, rendered headless in WebGL.
  • Brick Ninja: the ten side-scrolling worlds, with jumping, stars and sword.
  • Animal Sanctuary: the five wild zones, walking up to creatures and playing the befriending timing game.

The game's own logs stand in for the raycaster's NPCs, coins and shots, so every model fits exactly as before. Gaze targets are enemies or creatures on screen, items are soldiers, studs or creatures, and outcomes are bursts, stars or catch presses. Each game is compared with the raycaster at a matched 30 and 60 minutes.

The game matters, and every real game beats the raycaster on contrast response. At a matched hour with eye-tracker gaze, effective c50 goes from — on the raycaster to — on Betamax, — on Brick Ninja and — on Animal Sanctuary. The CRF exponent goes from — to —, — and —. Betamax is best overall. Its glare, haze, silhouettes and muzzle flash swing V1's contrast drive over the widest range, and its firefight gives the most salient events (attention gain — against —).

Animal Sanctuary's slow, flat pixel art costs it SF peak (—) and transient weight (—). Its constant creature encounters help attention, though (—). Outside V1, reward prediction error follows the number of outcomes. The two action games, at about 40 shots a minute, reach — (Betamax) and — (Brick Ninja). Animal Sanctuary's dozen catch presses a minute leave it level with the raycaster (— against —). The 2D games can't map self-motion. They have no forward flow at all, and their scrolling barely varies in direction: rotation preference reaches — on Brick Ninja against — on the raycaster.

Side notes

Game mechanics that help model estimates

Running notes: what limited each model, and the mechanic that would fix it. The full version, with examples from other games, is MECHANICS.md in the repository.

Direction codes

Grid and facing-direction codes need movement and heading in every direction. Any free 2D or 3D game works; a side-scroller does not. Grid codes from eye movements need smooth pursuit across the screen in all directions: birds, drones, tracer fire.

Distance to goal

Path and straight-line distance only come apart when walls force detours. That needs goals behind obstacles, locked doors and keycards (Doom), shortcuts that open later (Dark Souls), and rooms with several ways in (Prey, Dishonored).

Event boundaries

Keep them separate from rewards and explosions, or the arousal model removes them. Good boundaries are doors, room and chapter transitions, checkpoints and new objectives.

Decisions

Behaviour wants many decisions, but BOLD wants them spaced out, jittered and varied in difficulty. One example is friend-or-foe silhouettes in fog at a range of distances, a few seconds apart, like The Palace's standing figures.

Learning

Learning rates need many outcomes per minute, and payoffs that drift or reverse: weapons that jam, routes or loot whose value changes.

Memory for places

Needs many entries into places, many of them revisits after varied delays. Backtracking through known space with something changed (Metroidvania loops, keycard returns) gives this.

Puzzle elements for Betamax

Betamax is the best stimulus for vision, attention and reward, but its open beach starves the navigation and memory models. Candidates, each kept inside the battle (forward mode, no possession):

  • Several routes to every objective (Prey, Dishonored)
  • Ammunition or codebook return trips (Doom keycards)
  • Shortcuts that open after a house is cleared (Dark Souls)
  • A tide that closes the beach route and opens the boardwalk (The Palace's Architect, Antichamber)
  • Friend-or-foe silhouettes in the haze
  • A night phase where the flare dies and you get back to the bunker in the dark (The Palace's lantern)
  • An optional radio-code puzzle (Dishonored 2's Jindosh lock)

Built so far (Experiment 9): tunnels with several routes, ammunition runs, cave-ins, blackouts and friend-or-foe figures. They work for navigation, but the bot spent over half the hour below ground, which cost vision and reward learning. Cave-ins and figures were met too rarely for BOLD.

Tuning the tunnels

Tunnel time is borrowed from the battle, so keep trips short and make every one count:

  • Short connectors between fights rather than errands: under a quarter of the hour below ground keeps the beach's vision and reward signal
  • Cave-ins that persist across lives and sit on the routes people use, so a changed layout is met again and the update rate can be estimated
  • A figure every 20–40 s below ground, at mixed distances and lighting
  • Blackouts on the way back from the magazine, so every one is a homing trial
  • Lit, high-contrast tunnels (lamps, painted bands, daylight through the hatches) so V1 still has something to fit

How the simulation works

How the simulation works

A numpy raycaster first-person game (rooms, textured walls, NPCs, explosions, coins, probabilistic shots, covert-attention cues) is rendered at 10 Hz on a 24×18° screen. Each frame becomes multi-band contrast energy, shifted into retinal coordinates by a fixation/saccade gaze model. Planted populations: V1 voxels with pRFs, SF tuning, a contrast response with surround normalization, a transient channel and attention gain; numerosity, self-motion and striatal RPE voxels driven by the game's logs. Fits use eye-tracker gaze (offset, drift, gain error, noise, blinks), pRFs from a 15-min bar localizer, the canonical HRF shape with a fitted per-voxel time scale, and nuisance regressors for drift and logged arousal events. Dedicated experiments (band-pass noise, contrast series, discs/annuli, refresh rates, attention blocks, dots, optic flow, a reward task) are simulated in the same voxels, at 10, 30 and 60 minutes each.

Best case for model form: the fitter knows each model's functional form, not its parameters. Not simulated: head motion, spatially correlated noise, separate cardiac and respiratory components. Code, noise model (NOISE.md) and CSVs: github.com/samuelling/boldblaster.

Signal, noise and what the fitter removes

All noise is in percent signal change at TR = 1.5 s, independent per voxel. Gameplay keeps V1 driven but modulates any one pRF far less than bars do, which is why hours matter.

With noise removed, gameplay recovers pRFs to —°: the information is there, and the bottleneck is signal-to-noise. The noise model is moderate (about tSNR 110) and leaves out head motion and spatially correlated noise, so real data will need more time.
