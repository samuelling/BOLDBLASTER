What am I looking at?

What it shows

BOLDBLASTER is a simulation. We play a first-person game, plant known tuning parameters in simulated voxels (the ground truth), generate BOLD with realistic noise, and fit the parameters back out exactly as one would with real data. Because the truth is known, we can score how well each experimental design recovers it.

How to read it

The tiles compare the best gameplay result with the classic dedicated experiment for the same parameter (10 minutes, built for that one quantity). Spearman r measures whether voxels are put in the right order: 0.9 means the fitted values rank voxels almost exactly as the truth does; 0.3 means only a weak trend.

Signal vs noise

Treat differences under about 0.1 as noise for the 300-voxel rounds and under about 0.15 for the 120-voxel round 4. The sections below show 95% intervals so any comparison can be checked.

Caveats

Everything here is simulated. The fitter knows each model's functional form (not its parameters), and some real-world noise sources are not simulated; see section 05.

01 · Recovery

Which design recovers which parameter?

Each row is one design: a dedicated experiment built for that parameter, the game with and without design levers, and up to 20 hours across ten levels. Dots are Spearman r; whiskers are bootstrap 95% intervals over voxels (not computed for the non-visual populations).

TL;DR

Gameplay wins where a game naturally varies a quantity a lot or produces many events: transient responses (saccades, camera turns), numerosity (coin clusters) and reward outcomes. It needs hours, design levers or both to match dedicated experiments on SF tuning and attention. Parameters that interact (surround suppression, transient weight, CRF exponent) are barely recoverable one experiment at a time, so fitting them jointly is what matters.

What am I looking at?

What it shows

For the parameter you pick, one row per experimental design and the Spearman r between planted and fitted values across voxels.

  • Dedicated experiment: the classic design built for that parameter (band-pass noise for pSFT, a contrast series for pCRF, discs and annuli for surround suppression, refresh rates for the transient channel, attend-left/right blocks for attention, dot arrays, optic-flow blocks, a reward task), 10 minutes, fitting only that parameter with the others fixed at population defaults.
  • Battery fit jointly: all five V1 experiments (50 min) fitted together with every parameter free.
  • Game: 1–2 h of the round-2 game. Game + design levers: the round-3 game with covert-attention cues and SF/contrast-decorrelated textures.
  • Oracle: the same data fitted with true gaze and true pRFs, an upper bound on what better eye tracking and localizers would buy.
  • Ten levels: round 4, 2–20 h across ten level types on a 120-voxel subset (its dedicated row is re-scored on those same voxels).

How to read it

Dots further right recover the parameter better. Horizontal lines separate rounds. Hover any row for minutes of data, voxel count and the interval.

Signal vs noise

Whiskers are bootstrap 95% intervals over voxels. The shaded column is the dedicated experiment's interval: a design whose whisker sits entirely outside it is convincingly better or worse; overlap means treat it as a tie. As a rule of thumb, r is uncertain by about ±0.1 with 300 voxels and ±0.15 with 120.

Caveats

Compare within a round where possible (rounds 2–3 use 300 voxels, round 4 uses 120). Numerosity, self-motion and striatal rows have no intervals because their per-voxel fits were not saved, and their dedicated runs had 10 min against 60–120 min of play.

What am I looking at?

What it shows

Spearman r for the chosen parameter as sessions accumulate: 2 h is session 1 (Warehouse), 5 h adds Night ops and half of Open arena, 10 h is five levels, 20 h all ten. Orange is gameplay with its 95% band; the blue dashed line and band are the dedicated 10-minute experiment on the same 120 voxels.

How to read it

Where the orange line rises above the blue band, gameplay has overtaken the dedicated experiment. A line that keeps rising means more hours still help; a flat line means something other than data quantity is limiting.

Signal vs noise

Trust the overall trend from 2 to 10 to 20 h. Single steps can wobble: the 5 h point adds a dim, low-contrast level (Night ops), and with 120 voxels each point is uncertain by roughly ±0.15, so a dip of 0.1 between neighbours is not a real reversal.

Caveats

One simulated subject. Each session also has its own response gain and noise level, and the fitter estimates one gain per session.

Caveats

This does not test generalization (fitting on some levels and predicting an unseen one) or models whose variables one level under-samples, such as the range of coin counts. Variety may pay off there.

What am I looking at?

What it shows

One dot per simulated V1 voxel: its planted (true) value on the x-axis and the value fitted from the chosen design's data on the y-axis. Colour is eccentricity. Units: pSFT peak in log2 cycles/degree, bandwidth in octaves, effective c50 in contrast-energy units (log axes), CRF exponent unitless, surround suppression and transient weight as weights, attention as a fractional gain.

How to read it

  • Along the dashed diagonal: recovered.
  • Diagonal but tilted flatter: shrinkage, fitted values pulled toward the middle (typical when the data are weak).
  • Horizontal smear: the data barely constrain the parameter.
  • Rows of dots at the top or bottom edge: fits hit a parameter bound.

Use the eccentricity filter to see whether failures concentrate in the fovea or periphery.

Signal vs noise

The r above the plot is for all voxels of this design and parameter, from the results file; the filter changes what you see, not that number. A visible trend among a few dozen voxels in one eccentricity band can easily be chance.

Caveats

Effective c50 is c50 / (1+k)1/q, the half-saturation point a contrast experiment can actually measure; raw c50 trades off with surround suppression and the exponent.

05 · Assumptions

Signal, noise and what the fitter removes

All noise is in percent signal change at TR = 1.5 s, independent per voxel. Gameplay keeps V1 driven but modulates any one pRF far less than bars do, which is why hours matter.

TL;DR

Gameplay keeps V1 driven but modulates any one voxel only —% (SD over time), against —% for bars, below both the arousal signal (—%) and the noise (—%). With noise removed, gameplay recovers pRFs to —°: the information is there, and the bottleneck is signal-to-noise. The noise model is moderate (about tSNR 110) and leaves out head motion and spatially correlated noise, so real data will need more time.

What am I looking at?

What it shows

How big each part of a simulated voxel's time series is, as its standard deviation over time, median across 500 round-1 voxels: the visual response to bars, the visual response to gameplay, the arousal-linked systemic signal during gameplay, and the measurement noise.

How to read it

The ratio of a visual bar to the noise bar is the per-voxel signal-to-noise. Gameplay's visual bar is about a quarter of bars', so each minute of play carries far less information about a pRF than a minute of bars; that is why the hours curve in section 02 matters.

Signal vs noise

These are medians; individual voxels vary (peripheral voxels get relatively more gameplay modulation). The systemic signal is shared across voxels, so an event-locked regressor removes most of it; the noise is not removable.

Caveats

Round 1 used a sustained-only V1. The transient channel added in later rounds raises gameplay's modulation (see the table beside this).

What am I looking at?

What it shows

Every noise and nuisance component in the simulation, where it applies, its form and parameters, whether it is shared across voxels, and what the fitter does about it.

How to read it

Read across a row: a component the fitter models (drift, logged arousal, HRF timing, session gain) costs little; one it does not model (measurement noise, the slow global fluctuation) goes straight into the error. The last row lists what is not simulated at all.

Signal vs noise

Measurement noise of 0.6–1.2% per TR (AR(1), ρ = 0.3) is about tSNR 110: plausible for 7T at about 2 mm or a good 3T voxel, while many 3T voxels are noisier. The hours needed scale roughly with noise variance, so doubling the noise SD roughly quadruples the time.

Caveats

Independent noise per voxel, no head motion and no separate cardiac or respiratory signals make this an optimistic noise model. Full details: NOISE.md in the repository.

How the simulation works

A numpy raycaster first-person game (rooms, textured walls, NPCs, explosions, coins, probabilistic shots, covert-attention cues) is rendered at 10 Hz on a 24×18° screen. Each frame becomes multi-band contrast energy, shifted into retinal coordinates by a fixation/saccade gaze model. Planted populations: V1 voxels with pRFs, SF tuning, a contrast response with surround normalization, a transient channel and attention gain; numerosity, self-motion and striatal RPE voxels driven by the game's logs. Fits use eye-tracker gaze (offset, drift, gain error, noise, blinks), pRFs from a 15-min bar localizer, the canonical HRF shape with a fitted per-voxel time scale, and nuisance regressors for drift and logged arousal events. Dedicated experiments (band-pass noise, contrast series, discs/annuli, refresh rates, attention blocks, dots, optic flow, a reward task) are simulated in the same voxels.

Best case for model form: the fitter knows each model's functional form, not its parameters. Not simulated: head motion, spatially correlated noise, separate cardiac and respiratory components. Code, noise model (NOISE.md) and CSVs: github.com/samuelling/boldblaster.
